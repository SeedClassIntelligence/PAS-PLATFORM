ASG Reentry Demonstration Framework — Five-Phase Sentencing Intercept
Upstream Housing Prevention System · Dual-Use Architecture

A five-phase, thirteen-step intervention architecture operating across both prevention (COIP point-of-sentencing) and post-release reentry. The framework does not change — only the timing shifts. Applied upstream, it prevents housing loss at sentencing. Applied downstream, it governs reentry stabilization after release. Both applications produce the same outcome: sustained housing stability.

1
Sentencing Intercept (0–72 hours)
Mandatory Child Impact Assessment at sentencing. Family Navigator assigned within 24 hours. Immediate housing continuity assessment and caregiver contact. Benefits enrollment initiated. This is where housing instability is prevented — not addressed after the fact.
2
Acute Family Stabilization (First 30 Days)
Housing and income continuity secured. Emergency rent assistance where needed. Caregiver workforce connection initiated. Trauma screening and support activated. TANF, SNAP, and Medicaid enrollment completed.
3
Sustained Support During Incarceration
Quarterly judicial review hearings monitoring housing stability across all agencies. Caregiver workforce development and income advancement tracked. Pre-apprenticeship enrollment where applicable. Housing tenure monitoring.
4
Pre-Release Reunification Preparation (Final 6 Months)
Housing pre-arranged for returning parent. Employment pathway confirmed through KG Development or partner employer. Family counseling and reunification planning. Benefits continuity secured.
5
Post-Release Reintegration (12–24 Months)
Dual-income household stabilization. Wage progression tracking. Housing tenure confirmation at 6, 12, and 24 months. Long-term case closure criteria: sustained housing, employment, and family integrity for 24 consecutive months.

The Problem — Invisible at the Moment That Matters Most

In the United States, 2.6–2.7 million children currently have a parent in jail or prison, and nearly 5 million children—approximately 1 in 14—have experienced parental incarceration at some point in childhood. Despite the scale, predictability, and lifelong consequences of this harm, there is no national protection category, no mandatory federal intervention, and no coordinated response.

Mass incarceration is not confined to the justice system. It is a system-wide generator of harm that directly feeds the very institutions society claims to care about: foster care, juvenile education, youth homelessness, behavioral health, special education, juvenile justice, and aging-out pipelines. Children of incarcerated parents move predictably through these systems not because of personal failure, but because the system has already failed them.

A Solution Group CDC names what policy has refused to acknowledge: Mass incarceration is a child-welfare crisis—and the absence of intervention is actively producing the next generation of system-involved youth and adults.

When a parent is sentenced, courts record the existence of children—yet trigger no required stabilization, protection, or monitoring. This moment is where family destabilization becomes predictable and where children become invisible inputs into multiple failing systems.

When a parent is sentenced, courts document the existence of children yet trigger no required stabilization, protection, or monitoring. That omission initiates a predictable cascade: educational disruption, housing instability, untreated trauma, foster care entry, juvenile system contact, and eventual adult justice involvement.

Without Intervention at Sentencing

Absent intervention at sentencing, children impacted by parental incarceration appear repeatedly in:

  • Foster care and emergency placement systems
  • Behavioral and mental health services
  • Special education and school discipline pathways

Yet no single system claims responsibility for preventing the harm.

The Timing Gap

Current reentry policy activates support at release—often 2–10 years after sentencing. By that time:

  • Children have already cycled through foster care, juvenile justice, or homelessness

Most social, public, and private systems fail not because of lack of effort, funding, or expertise—but because interventions are fragmented, mistimed, and disconnected from human reality.

The origin insight behind this architecture came from a single, repeatable failure point: when systems encounter predictable harm, they document it—but fail to intervene at the moment it matters most.

This failure is most visible in the justice system. When a parent is sentenced, courts routinely record the existence of children yet trigger no required stabilization, protection, or monitoring. The result is predictable downstream collapse across child welfare, education, behavioral health, housing, and workforce systems.

A Solution Group CDC translated this insight into a transferable operating intelligence—not limited to justice—that corrects mistimed intervention across any system lifecycle.

"The architecture stewarded here is not a collection of programs. It is a transferable operating intelligence designed to: identify root generators instead of symptoms; intervene at the correct moment in the lifecycle of harm; execute with human precision at scale; repair trust and capacity so systems actually hold."

While born from community health, justice, and child welfare, this architecture is industry-agnostic and deployable wherever human systems break down.
