TIMING
Page 02 · The Problem We Solve
Every Housing Stabilization
Program in America Is Late
Not underfunded. Not poorly designed. Structurally late. The moment they activate — at release, at shelter entry, at eviction — the damage has already cascaded through families, children, schools, and communities. This is the problem the Advent model is designed to correct.
The Scale of the Problem

In the United States, 2.7 million children currently have a parent in jail or prison. Approximately 1 in 14 children will experience parental incarceration at some point during childhood. Despite the scale, predictability, and lifelong consequences of this harm, there is no national protection category, no mandatory federal intervention, and no coordinated response at the moment it begins.

This is not a gap in resources. It is a gap in timing. Every system that encounters the downstream consequences — foster care, emergency housing, juvenile justice, behavioral health, workforce instability — spends enormous resources responding to harm that was predictable from the moment a parent was sentenced. No system intervenes at that moment.

"When a parent is sentenced, courts record the existence of children — yet trigger no required housing stabilization, benefit enrollment, or family monitoring. That single omission produces a predictable cascade that every downstream system is forced to absorb."

— ASG CDC Cross-System Impact Analysis
The Cascade — What Happens Without Intervention

Children of incarcerated parents do not fail because of personal deficits. They fail because systems have already failed them. The sequence is predictable, documented, and preventable:

  1. Parent is sentenced. Courts document the existence of dependent children. No intervention is triggered. The family is on its own from this moment forward.
  2. Housing destabilizes immediately. Income is lost or reduced. Rent goes unpaid. Eviction proceedings begin — often within 30 to 60 days. Children move between family members, neighbors, emergency shelter. Stability ends.
  3. Educational continuity breaks. Children change schools when housing changes. Academic progress halts. Behavioral issues emerge. Special education pathways are entered. Graduation becomes less likely.
  4. Behavioral health crises emerge. Trauma from sudden parental loss, housing instability, and caregiver stress goes unaddressed. Children develop anxiety, depression, PTSD. Mental health services are not coordinated. Crises escalate.
  5. Foster care entry occurs. When caregiver stability collapses, children enter the foster care system. The average cost: $150,000 to $250,000 over three years. The damage to the child: irreversible without intensive intervention.
  6. Juvenile justice contact follows. Children without stable housing, education, or mental health support are exponentially more likely to enter the juvenile justice system. The cycle begins its next generation.
  7. 2–10 years later, the parent is released. Traditional reentry systems activate now. By this point, the family has already cycled through multiple failing systems. The housing the parent returns to does not exist. The child has already been damaged. The "reentry program" is managing consequences it could have prevented.
The Cross-System Cost

Children entering one at-risk system due to parental incarceration show a 40–85% probability of entering additional systems. The downstream cost over 18 years, per family, is documented at approximately $1.36 million across foster care, behavioral health, special education, emergency housing, juvenile justice, and adult criminal justice.

Why Housing Is the Right Frame

Housing is not the only outcome affected by parental incarceration — but it is the hinge point. When housing destabilizes, everything else follows. When housing is preserved, families have a platform from which every other intervention becomes possible. Education works better with stable housing. Employment is more accessible. Behavioral health improves. Children stay in school.

The Advent model treats housing stability as the primary outcome — not a byproduct of employment, not a result of sobriety, not a consequence of compliance. Housing stability is the mission. Employment and the sentencing intercept are the mechanisms that make it durable.

The Advent model is not an incremental improvement on existing housing programs. It is a structural correction to the timing failure that all existing programs share. Three specific innovations distinguish it from every other model currently operating in the field.
Innovation 1 — The Sentencing Intercept
Activating at the Moment of Predictable Risk — Not After Collapse

No existing housing program activates at sentencing. Emergency shelters, housing vouchers, rapid rehousing, and transitional housing all activate after housing has been lost — days, months, or years after the moment when intervention was still possible at low cost and high impact.

The COIP sentencing intercept changes the activation point. Within 0–72 hours of sentencing, a Mandatory Child Impact Assessment is completed and a Family Navigator is assigned. Within 30 days, housing continuity is confirmed, benefits are enrolled, and trauma screening is underway. The family never loses housing — so it never needs emergency housing. Children never enter foster care as a consequence of housing loss. The downstream cascade never begins.

This is the innovation that makes every other component of the Advent model possible. Without it, workforce development and housing construction are downstream interventions that arrive after the damage. With it, they become part of a prevention architecture that holds families together from the beginning.

Innovation 2 — Developer-Anchored Employment
Employment Is Not Promised. It Is Guaranteed.

Most workforce development programs train participants and release them into a job market. Placement rates vary. Employment quality varies. Retention collapses without structural support. The innovation in the Advent model is not training — it is the guaranteed employment pipeline anchored to an active construction project.

Participants who complete the 12-week OSHA training program on the Advent UMC site are placed directly onto the construction site — not into a job search. Housing stability is secured not by subsidizing rent, but by generating income that sustains rent.

The wage progression model — $18/hr at entry, $28/hr within 24 months — combined with union apprenticeship pathways through the Carpenters, IBEW, and UA, means that participants are not just housed. They are building careers in a sector that offers sustained middle-class income.

COIP
Page 07 · COIP Architecture — The Upstream Engine
The Point-of-Sentencing
Housing Stabilization System
The COIP sentencing intercept is the mechanism that makes the Advent model a housing prevention system rather than a housing response program. It is not an add-on. It is the root-cause correction — the intervention that stops the cascade before it starts. This page documents the full architecture, the five phases, and the judicial accountability structure.
The Core Insight
Housing Instability Has a Predictable Trigger. The System Has Been Ignoring It.

Every downstream housing instability that costs governments, communities, and families enormous resources is preceded by a traceable upstream event. For the 2.7 million children with incarcerated parents, that event is sentencing. The moment a parent is sentenced, housing destabilization becomes statistically predictable. Yet at that exact moment, no system intervenes.

Courts document the existence of dependent children at sentencing. They do not activate stabilization. Child welfare agencies do not receive automatic notification. Housing authorities are not alerted. Benefits navigation does not begin. The family is left to manage on its own — and in the majority of cases, it cannot.

The COIP sentencing intercept inserts a mandatory, structured response at that exact moment. What follows is not a new program — it is a timing correction applied to an existing crisis that every system is already paying for.

The Five-Phase Architecture

The ASG Reentry Demonstration Framework defines a five-phase, 13-step implementation system for the COIP sentencing intercept. Each phase has specific actions, responsible parties, accountability structures, and outcome metrics. The phases below represent the full deployment model — not a simplified summary.

1
Phase 1 · 0–72 Hours Post-Sentencing
Sentencing Intercept — Mandatory Child Impact Assessment

At sentencing, a Mandatory Child Impact Assessment is completed for all defendants with dependent minor children. The assessment identifies all dependent children, their current living situation, primary caregiver capacity, immediate financial risks, housing status, and benefit enrollment gaps. Within 24 hours of sentencing, a Family Navigator — a trained Community Health Worker with lived experience — is assigned as the single point of coordination for the family. The Navigator makes immediate contact with the caregiver, conducts a needs assessment, and begins housing continuity verification.

Accountability structure: Navigator reports to the court via quarterly judicial review hearings. The judge maintains oversight across the incarceration period — not just at sentencing and release.

Mandatory Child Impact Assessment at sentencingFamily Navigator assigned within 24 hoursCourt oversight activated immediately
2
Phase 2 · First 30 Days
Acute Family Stabilization

Within the first 30 days, the Family Navigator secures housing continuity — meaning the family is confirmed in stable housing and not at immediate risk of eviction. Caregiver income stability is assessed and emergency support activated where necessary. Benefits enrollment is completed: TANF, SNAP, Medicaid, and any applicable housing assistance. Initial trauma screening is completed for children and caregivers. School stability is confirmed — children remain in the same school unless relocation is in their best interest. Employment and benefit continuity for the incarcerated parent's household are stabilized to the extent possible.

Housing continuity confirmed within 30 daysTANF · SNAP · Medicaid enrolledSchool stability confirmed
3
Phase 3 · Duration of Incarceration
Sustained Support — Quarterly Judicial Accountability

Every 90 days during the incarceration period, a court-ordered judicial review hearing convenes with the judge, the Family Navigator, the caregiver, and child welfare representatives where relevant. The hearing reviews housing stability, children's school and behavioral health status, caregiver employment and income, and any emerging risks. The Navigator coordinates across child welfare, education, healthcare, and community partners throughout this period — not just at quarterly reviews. Visitation is facilitated where safe and therapeutically appropriate. Ongoing trauma-informed support is maintained for children and caregivers.

Quarterly judicial review hearingsCross-agency coordination throughoutTrauma-informed support maintained
4
Phase 4 · Final 6 Months Pre-Release
Reunification Preparation

Beginning six months before the projected release date, the Family Navigator coordinates a structured reunification preparation process. Housing is confirmed pre-release — meaning the returning parent knows exactly where they are going before they walk out the door, not after they are released into homelessness. Employment is confirmed or actively arranged — in the Advent model, this means placement in the KG Development workforce pipeline is confirmed before release, so the first day out is also the first day of work. Parenting preparation and family counseling are provided. Child welfare oversight is maintained through the transition. Benefit continuity is ensured so there is no gap in coverage at release.

5
Phase 5 · 12–24 Months Post-Release
Full Reintegration — Verified Case Closure

For 12–24 months following release, the Family Navigator maintains intensive monitoring and stabilization support. Housing and employment stability are tracked. Children's school and behavioral health status are monitored. The family is connected to community supports — mutual aid, peer networks, cultural programming, and civic engagement opportunities. Case closure requires verified satisfaction of specific criteria: stable housing for minimum 12 months, sustained employment with documented wage progression, children confirmed in stable educational and behavioral health situations, and family self-assessed readiness for reduced support. There is no administrative case closure based on a time limit.

The Family Navigator Role
The Human Infrastructure That Carries the Model

The Family Navigator is the operational core of the COIP architecture. This is a Community Health Worker (CHW) role with a specific profile: lived experience with the justice system or parental incarceration, training in trauma-informed care and systems navigation, and assignment to a specific family from the moment of sentencing through case closure. The Navigator is not a caseworker who rotates families. They are a consistent human relationship — often the first stable, trustworthy institutional contact a family has experienced.

The CHW certification pipeline — defined in Section 13 and Appendix D of the ASG Reentry Demonstration Framework — establishes specific training requirements, scope-of-practice boundaries, supervision structures, and advancement pathways. The pipeline progresses from entry-level CHW (40 hours initial training) through Peer Specialist (120 hours) and CHW Supervisor (240 hours) to Solutionologist Candidate (480 hours) — creating a workforce development track within the service delivery model itself.

  • Living wage minimums with quarterly compliance tracking — not minimum wage compliance
  • Entry-level to supervisory advancement guarantees in writing, with timelines specified
  • Local business procurement targets at 40% minimum for materials and subcontracting
  • Bonus incentives for developers who exceed hiring and diversity goals
  • Community resident oversight of developer performance through accountability structure
Replication Engine 2 — WCS Licensing Structure
Section 15: Methodology Fidelity That Prevents Scale from Becoming Dilution

The most common failure mode of scaled social programs is dilution — the model spreads to new sites but loses its essential elements along the way. Practitioners are trained inadequately, nine pillars are reduced to three, the accountability structures are removed because they are inconvenient, and the program that produces results at one site produces nothing at five others.

Section 15 of the ASG Reentry Demonstration Framework is specifically designed to prevent this. The WCS Licensing Compliance Framework establishes:

  • Territorial license specifications: Primary territory (Las Vegas/Southern Nevada) with defined expansion rights requiring originator approval. Geographic expansion is not automatic — it is earned through demonstrated fidelity at existing sites
  • WCS framework attribution: Proper credit to William Darnell Jernigan IV as WCS Framework originator is required at all sites. This is not vanity — it is the accountability chain that links every site back to the framework's quality standards
  • Methodology fidelity requirements: The nine-pillar integration is non-negotiable. Sites may adapt delivery to local cultural contexts and populations — but they may not reduce the nine-pillar structure or remove community control elements
  • Prohibited modifications: Framework dilution (reducing nine-pillar integration), attribution removal, and commercial exploitation without explicit permission are contractually prohibited at all licensed sites

The licensing structure means that when ASG CDC certifies a Solutionologist in a new city, that person is deploying the same model — not a local interpretation of it. The model scales without losing what makes it work.

Community Wealth Building
Beyond Individual Families — Neighborhood and Community Transformation

The Advent model does not just stabilize families. At scale, it produces structural changes in the communities where it operates:

  • Community land trust development: The Advent UMC property transition to community ownership is the first example of a broader strategy of acquiring distressed properties and converting them to community-held assets through rehabilitation work performed by program participants
  • Cooperative business development: Participants who advance to supervisory roles are supported in launching cooperative businesses — shared ownership enterprises that generate ongoing income for resident stakeholders rather than extracting value to outside owners
  • Local economic circulation: The 40% local procurement requirement means that developer spending circulates within the community rather than flowing to distant suppliers — creating multiplier effects in neighborhood economies
  • Intergenerational knowledge transfer: The Solutionologist network's mentorship structure ensures that skills, credentials, and community knowledge are passed from experienced practitioners to the next generation of community leaders — not captured in institutional silos
  • Civic engagement and resident governance: The community-controlled data and evaluation system gives residents real authority over program performance assessment — not just feedback mechanisms that are ignored
